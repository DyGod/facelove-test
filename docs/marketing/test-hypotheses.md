# Hypotheses

Why we made each conversion change. Each entry says what we believed, what we changed because of it, and what would prove us wrong. We don't run a formal test for every entry. These notes are here so the reasoning isn't lost.

## H1. One Black Friday sale block instead of two

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`.

The buy box showed the Black Friday sale twice. The countdown ("Price increases in") sat above the bundle picker. A second block with a progress bar ("Selling fast", 87%) and another countdown sat below the add to cart button. They said the same thing and pushed the guarantee and gift list further apart.

We kept the top one and removed the bottom one.

- The shopper picks a bundle and price right under the top timer. A deadline next to the price makes the deal feel real at the moment they choose. The bottom block showed up after that choice, mostly to people already scrolling past.
- On mobile the bottom block sat a full screen below the button. Most shoppers never reached it before they bought or left.
- The bottom block's 87% bar was a number we typed in, not real stock. It carried the same trust and legal risk as the "Low stock" badge we removed the same day.

What we expect: conversion rate holds or rises slightly, because the guarantee now sits closer to the button.

What would prove us wrong: conversion rate on this page drops in the two weeks after the change, compared with the two weeks before.

Open item. The top timer says "Price increases in". That has to be true. If the price does not go up on 2026-12-01, change the label to "Sale ends in".

## H2. Show the free gifts once, inside the bundle card

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`.

Free shipping, the mystery gift and the primer showed in two places. One list sat inside the 2 stick card in the bundle picker. A row of tiles sat below the add to cart button, greyed out with a lock until the shopper picked the 2 stick bundle.

We kept the list inside the card and removed the tiles.

- The card is where the shopper compares 1 stick with 2 sticks. Seeing the gifts there makes the bigger bundle look like the better deal at the moment of choice. The tiles came after the button, once the choice was already made.
- The card list is the one that adds the gifts to the cart. With auto-add on, picking the 2 stick card puts the primer and mystery gift in the cart for free. The tiles were only a picture. Removing the card list would have meant shoppers no longer got their gifts.
- The two lists said the same thing in two styles, which made the buy box longer and harder to scan on mobile.

What we expect: the share of orders on the 2 stick bundle holds or rises, and more shoppers see the guarantee because it sits right under the button.

What would prove us wrong: the share of 2 stick orders drops in the two weeks after the change. The locked tiles may have nudged 1 stick shoppers to switch. If that happens, put the locked tiles back above the button instead of below it.

## H3. Serif headings in sentence case

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`. The font setting is store-wide.

Every heading on the page was Montserrat Bold, and the sale lines and buttons were in capital letters. The page read like a discount ad. A thin, high-contrast serif for headlines with a plain sans-serif for body text and prices is the pairing shoppers know from department store beauty counters.

We changed the theme's heading font to Cormorant Medium and set headings in normal capitalisation. Headings are about 20% larger to make up for the thinner strokes.

- The product title and section headings are what shoppers read while scrolling. A serif makes the product look like it costs more than $39, so the sale price reads as a bigger saving.
- Our shoppers are women over 40. Larger headings in normal capitalisation are easier to read than small bold capitals.
- Prices, buttons and body text stay in Montserrat, so nothing in the purchase steps changed shape.

What we expect: conversion rate holds or rises. Time on page may rise a little.

What would prove us wrong: conversion rate drops in the two weeks after the change. If it does, look at headings on mobile first, where the serif is smallest.

## H4. A calmer buy box

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`.

Almost every line in the buy box was bold: the benefit pills, both sale lines, the bundle names and prices, the gift lines and the button. When everything is bold, nothing stands out, and the block looks crowded.

We lowered the weight of everything except the button and the prices, and changed the black and lilac accents to one deep plum.

- With lighter text around it, the add to cart button is the strongest thing in the buy box. Shoppers find it faster.
- One accent color for the sale bar and the button ties the deadline to the action.
- "Add to cart" in normal capitalisation matches the new headings. Capital letters read as shouting next to a serif.

What we expect: add to cart rate holds or rises.

What would prove us wrong: add to cart rate drops, or the share of 2 stick orders drops because the lighter gift lines are easier to miss. If the 2 stick share drops, put the gift lines back to bold first.

## H5. Trust badges under the button

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`.

The money-back guarantee was in a grey box under the button. Free shipping and secure payment were only in the last section of the page, below the FAQ. Shoppers hesitate right after they pick a bundle and before they tap the button. That is where doubts about shipping cost, returns and payment come up.

We put three short trust lines and the payment icons directly under the button and turned off the separate guarantee box.

- Shoppers see their main reasons to say no answered right where they decide.
- Payment icons tell shoppers they can pay with a method they already use, such as PayPal or Apple Pay. Shoppers who don't know the brand check for this before they enter card details.
- The badges replace the guarantee box, so the buy box did not get longer.

What we expect: add to cart rate rises, and fewer shoppers leave at checkout.

What would prove us wrong: add to cart rate drops or holds flat over two weeks. A flat result would mean the bottom-of-page trust section was already enough, and we could remove the badges to shorten the buy box.

Risk. The payment icons are a typed list until the real store's payment methods are turned on. Showing a method the store does not accept would break trust at checkout and may break consumer protection rules. Check the list against the real store before launch.

## H6. Reviews before claims

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`.

The page went logos, comparison table, five reasons, then reviews. Shoppers read four screens of claims from us before they saw any proof from customers.

We moved the reviews to second place, right after the press logos.

- The before and after photos are the strongest proof on the page. Showing them early means shoppers read the comparison and the five reasons already believing the product works.
- Press logos followed by customer reviews puts two kinds of proof in a row, one from editors and one from customers.
- The logo strip lost its grey box and got thin lines instead, so it looks like a magazine masthead and leads straight into the reviews.

What we expect: more shoppers scroll past the reviews, and conversion rate rises.

What would prove us wrong: conversion rate drops, or fewer shoppers reach the comparison table. If so, the comparison may have been the hook, and we should swap the two back.

## H7. Ingredient spotlight

Shipped 2026-11-18. Page: `product.landing-01`.

The page said the stick is skincare and suits sensitive skin, but the only proof was the full ingredient list hidden in the FAQ. Most shoppers will not read a list of Latin ingredient names. Four named ingredients with a plain line each are easier to take in, and they show we are proud of the formula.

We added a section after the five reasons that names four ingredients from the real list, with one line each.

- It answers "will this irritate my skin?" before the shopper reaches the guarantee and FAQ.
- It backs up reason five ("skincare and anti-aging") with something specific.
- We only used claims the product page already makes. We did not add "dermatologist tested", "fragrance free" or similar, because we have no proof of them. Add them only with a test report or certificate on file.

What we expect: fewer shoppers open the sensitive skin and ingredient questions in the FAQ, and conversion rate holds or rises.

What would prove us wrong: conversion rate drops, or scroll depth past this section drops sharply. A sharp drop would mean the page is now too long, and this section should move into the FAQ area or be removed.
